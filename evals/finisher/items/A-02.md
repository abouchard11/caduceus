# Council Memo - 20260822-200125-27f347

**Question:** Should we implement feature flags for gradual rollouts?

**Domain:** implementation | **Mode:** quick->full | **Seats:** forge, finisher, recon, sparring | **Elapsed:** 198.6s | 2026-08-22 20:04

## Recommendation (weighted, not voted)

**Lead seat:** FORGE (weight 0.390 = believability x confidence 0.78)

Pick the single highest-risk rollout path—next model or prompt change on the most-trafficked AI product with live users—and gate it with exactly one flag using a self-hosted OpenFeature-compatible tool (GrowthBook or Unleash free tier). Attach a removal date (180 days default) and a lifecycle ledger entry (owner, expiry, on/off test status). Treat that one flag as the discipline experiment: if adding and removing it proves cheap, expand to other high-cost-revert products; if it rots or the ledger is not maintained, skip flags entirely and invest in trunk-based deploys with fast revert.

**Why:**

- Kill-switch value (instant rollback without redeploy) is independent of traffic volume and applies even on low-traffic products where gradual percentage rollouts are statistically meaningless.
- Per-product value equation: high-cost-revert products (AI products with paying users, model/prompt changes) justify flags; low-cost-revert products (static SEO sites, git-revert in minutes) do not.
- Flag rot is the dominant failure mode, but the mitigation is operational discipline (lifecycle contract with owner + expiry + tested states), not avoiding flags entirely.
- Infrastructure-level canary deploys are cleaner than application-level flags for gradual rollouts, but they don't provide the kill-switch value without redeployment.
- The constraint is operational discipline, not tooling cost—OpenFeature/GrowthBook/Unleash are cheap and vendor-neutral, but without a removal ritual they become dead code.

**Known risks in this recommendation:**

- The 'one flag as experiment' recommendation assumes the team will actually run the removal ritual. If the discipline proves too costly even for one flag, the entire approach fails.
- Per-product value equation requires accurate assessment of revert cost, which may be unclear until a bad deploy actually happens.
- Lifecycle contract (owner, expiry, tested states) adds overhead that may not be justified for very small teams or solo operators.
- Distinguishing kill-switch from gradual-rollout value may be unclear in practice—teams may implement both when only one is needed.

## Minority Report (preserved verbatim - read this)

**Dissenting seat:** FINISHER (weight 0.375, confidence 0.75)

Position: Conditional yes, scoped per product class: flags only as kill-switches — not statistical percentage ramps — and only where revert is genuinely slow or costly (AI products with paying users facing model/prompt swaps), each bound to an enforced lifecycle ledger: owner, expiry, on/off both tested, kill-switch verified at creation. Static/SEO sites with a fast git-revert path get zero flags. If nobody will fund and maintain the ledger, skip flags entirely and take the rollback value via trunk-based deploys with fast revert.

Recommendation: Create the flag lifecycle ledger first (tracked file: owner, expiry date, flag type, on/off test status, kill-switch-verified-at-creation) as the hard approval gate, then approve exactly one flag against it — a binary kill-switch for the next model/prompt change on his most-trafficked AI product, no percentage ramp until cohort telemetry exists. No second flag until the first is removed and the ledger has demonstrably survived one full cycle; if the ledger goes stale, abandon flags and take rollback via git-revert.

- The unique value of a flag is rollback without redeploy. Draft A's infra-canary alternative only covers regressions that manifest during the canary window; problems that surface after promotion (cron-triggered billing bug, slow model-quality drift) need an instant kill, and only a flag provides that.
- Flag debt is the dominant documented failure mode (Fowler, FeatureToggle; Knight Capital 2012, ~$440M from a stale repurposed flag re-activating a dead code path). Therefore the adoption gate is an enforced lifecycle contract, not a tooling choice — tooling is now free (OpenFeature, GrowthBook/Unleash OSS tiers), which makes operational discipline the binding constraint, as Draft B correctly notes.
- Adopting Draft B's per-product value equation: the decision key is revert cost. Bad deploy on a static SEO site = git-revert + redeploy in minutes, no flag justified; bad model/prompt swap on a paying-user AI product = churn, flag justified. A blanket policy in either direction is wrong for a mixed portfolio.
- Gradual percentage rollout without cohort telemetry is theater: at low-to-moderate traffic, 10% of the user base is anecdotal noise, not a canary (convergent point across Drafts A and B). Until traffic supports cohort comparison of error rate/latency/conversion against documented stop/go thresholds, use the flag as a binary kill-switch, not a statistical ramp.
- Mechanism over intention: N flags = 2^N runtime states, so every approved flag requires CI coverage of flag-on, flag-off, and flip-in-flight at creation, a ledger entry (owner, expiry, type, on/off test status, kill-switch verified) as the approval gate, a hard cap on active flags, and a recurring deletion job. Draft B's 'removal date' and Draft C's '30-day default' are intentions without enforcement and will rot.
- The cheaper default covers most cases: trunk-based development, small deploys, and fast revert buy most of the rollback value with far less state space. Flags are justified only where deploy risk or release cadence is genuinely high — which, for this portfolio, is one product class, not all of it.
